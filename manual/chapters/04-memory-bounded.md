# 4. Memory-bounded and external search {#ch-4}

[Source: `include/PBeFS/astar/IDAStar.h` (`IDAStar`, `idastar`),
`include/PBeFS/astar/DDDAStar.h` (`DDDAStar`, `DDDOptions`,
`DiskDomain`, `ddd_astar`), `include/PBeFS/astar/Domain.h`
(`OperatorDomain`, `for_each_child`), `include/PBeFS/astar/Solution.h`
(`SearchOptions`, `Status`); the tests `idastar`, `ddd` and `tiles24` of

We are concerned in this chapter with the searches that find an optimal
path when the nodes generated by A$^*$ do not fit in memory. A$^*$
([Chapter 2](02-best-first.md#ch-2)) stores all the nodes that it
generates, in OPEN or in CLOSED, and on the larger puzzles this is its
real limit: the 15-puzzle has $16!/2 \approx 1.05 \cdot 10^{13}$
reachable states, and Korf and Schultze needed disks and a parallel
breadth-first search to visit them all once
[[156]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-156). One can give up part of what A$^*$
keeps in several ways, and the chapter is organized by them. A search
may keep only a bounded number of nodes and accept to lose completeness
(beam search, [§4.2](#sec-4-2)), keep only the current path and give up
the detection of the duplicates (depth-first branch-and-bound and
iterative deepening, [§4.3](#sec-4-3) and [§4.4](#sec-4-4)), keep the
current path with the values backed up along it (recursive best-first
search, [§4.5](#sec-4-5)), keep everything but on the disk, detecting
the duplicates in batches ([§4.6](#sec-4-6)), represent sets of states
compactly as Boolean functions ([§4.7](#sec-4-7)), or prune the states
that bring nothing new in terms of features ([§4.8](#sec-4-8)). The
library implements IDA$^*$ and A$^*$ with delayed duplicate detection on
the disk; the other methods are described with their theory in sections
marked as planned. We use the notation of
[Chapter 1](01-introduction.md#ch-1) and the results on A$^*$ of
[Chapter 2](02-best-first.md#ch-2), in particular the invariant of OPEN
([Lemma 2.7](02-best-first.md#lem-2-7)) and the monotonicity of $f$
under a consistent heuristic ([Lemma 2.12](02-best-first.md#lem-2-12));
$\varepsilon > 0$ is a lower bound on the costs of the edges whenever
one is assumed, $b$ the branching factor and $d$ the number of edges of
a path.

## 4.1 What limits A$^*$ {#sec-4-1}

With an admissible heuristic A$^*$ expands (at least) every node with
$f < C^*$ when $h$ is consistent ([Theorem
2.14](02-best-first.md#thm-2-14)), and it stores, besides them, all the
successors that it has generated; the memory therefore grows as the
number of the generated nodes, i.e., roughly $b$ times the expansions.
In the library each node holds the state, $g$, $h$, the parent and a few
flags ([§3.7](03-data-structures.md#sec-3-7)), plus its place in CLOSED
and its entries in OPEN; with a state of one or two words this is some
tens of bytes per node, and a search that generates $10^{10}$ nodes
needs a memory that few machines have. The time of A$^*$, on the other
hand, usually remains acceptable well after its memory is exhausted,
since an expansion costs little more than the generation of its
successors and a few lookups. Hence the methods of this chapter trade
time for memory, and the table below summarizes what each one keeps and
what it guarantees.

| Method | Keeps | Optimal | Complete | In the library |
|:--|:--|:-:|:-:|:--|
| A$^*$ | OPEN and CLOSED | yes | yes | `AStar` |
| beam search | $k$ nodes per layer | no | no | planned |
| depth-first B&B | the path | finite tree | finite tree | trees only |
| DFID, IDA$^*$ | the path | yes | yes | `IDAStar` |
| RBFS | the path and the siblings | yes | yes | planned |
| external A$^*$ | OPEN and CLOSED on the disk | yes | yes | `DDDAStar` |
| symbolic search | sets of states as BDDs | yes | yes | planned |
| width-based search | tuples of features | no | no (IW$(i)$) | planned |

The column "Optimal" assumes an admissible heuristic (and, for the
external A$^*$, a consistent one), the depth-first branch-and-bound of
the library is that of the trees of
[Chapter 13](13-branch-and-bound.md#ch-13), and the conditions under
which each method terminates are given in its section (for RBFS, the
two marks are those claimed by its author, which we do not prove).

## 4.2 Beam search {#sec-4-2}

> **Status — planned.** The library has no beam search; this section
> states the method and its properties.

A beam search bounds the memory by keeping, at each layer of a
breadth-first search, only the $k$ nodes that are best according to an
evaluation, usually $h$ or $f$; the others are dropped for good.

<a id="def-4-1"></a>**Definition 4.1** (Beam search). Let $L_0 =
\{s_0\}$. For $d \ge 0$, let $S_d$ be the set of the successors of the
nodes of $L_d$, and let $L_{d+1}$ be the $k$ nodes of $S_d$ with the
least evaluation (all of them if they are at most $k$). The search stops
at the first layer that contains a goal, or when a layer is empty.

**Algorithm 4.1** (Breadth-first beam search of width $k$).

```text
1  procedure BEAM(s₀, k)
2     L ← {s₀}
3     while L ≠ ∅ do
4        if L contains a goal t then return the path to t
5        S ← the successors of the nodes of L
6        L ← the k nodes of S of least h (or f)
7     return failure
```

<a id="prop-4-2"></a>**Proposition 4.2** (Memory and incompleteness of
beam search). Algorithm 4.1 holds at most $k b$ nodes at a time, plus
the parents needed to rebuild the path, i.e., $O(k (b + d))$ nodes if
the goal is found at depth $d$; it is not complete, and when it finds a
goal the path need not be optimal.

*Proof.* A layer has at most $k$ nodes and each has at most $b$
successors; keeping the parents of the $d$ layers adds at most $k d$
nodes. For the incompleteness it suffices to drop, in some layer, the
only node from which a goal is reachable, as in
[Figure 4.1](#fig-4-1); for the optimality, a goal at depth $d$ may be
found before a cheaper one at a larger depth, since the layers are taken
by depth, whatever their cost. $\square$

![Figure 4.1. Breadth-first beam search of width $k = 2$ by $h$. In each
layer the two nodes of least $h$ are kept and the others are dropped
(rust); the goal $t$, a successor of the dropped node $c$, is never
generated, although it is the best node of the next
layer.](../figures/memory-bounded-beam.svg){#fig-4-1}

Also, the width $k$ trades the quality of the path for the memory, and a
beam search may also keep a closed list for the states of the beam,
which helps on graphs with many cycles; Edelkamp and Schrödl
[[10]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-10) describe this and the other variants
(e.g., beams on $f$ instead of layers).

## 4.3 Depth-first branch-and-bound {#sec-4-3}

> **Status — planned.** The depth-first branch-and-bound of a `Domain`
> is not in the library; the branch-and-bound searches of
> [Chapter 13](13-branch-and-bound.md#ch-13) offer it on a tree given by
> its branching, with the depth-first selection.

Depth-first branch-and-bound (DFBnB) searches the tree of the paths from
$s_0$ depth first, with the successors of a node taken by increasing $h$,
and prunes each node whose $f$ is not less than the cost $U$ of the best
goal found so far (the *incumbent*); the memory is that of the current
path and of the successors not yet tried along it.

**Algorithm 4.2** (Depth-first branch-and-bound).

```text
1  procedure DFBNB(u, g)
2     if g + h(u) ≥ U then return                           ▷ pruned
3     if u is a goal then U ← g;  record the path;  return
4     for each successor v of u, by increasing h(v) do
5        DFBNB(v, g + c(u, v))

   U ← ∞;  DFBNB(s₀, 0);  return the recorded path, if U < ∞
```

<a id="prop-4-3"></a>**Proposition 4.3** (Optimality and memory of
DFBnB). On a finite tree of depth $D$, with $h$ admissible, Algorithm
4.2 terminates, returns a path of cost $C^*$ if a goal exists, and holds
$O(b D)$ nodes at a time.

*Proof.* The tree is finite and each node is visited at most once,
hence the search terminates. Let $P$ be an optimal path; the nodes $u$ of
$P$ have $g(u) + h(u) \le C^*$, by admissibility. While $U > C^*$, no
node of $P$ is pruned at line 2, and the depth-first search, which
visits each node that it does not prune, reaches the goal of $P$ and
sets $U = C^*$; afterwards $U$ never increases, and a goal recorded
later would need $g < U$. Hence the recorded path costs $C^*$. The
recursion has at most $D + 1$ levels, each holding the at most $b$
successors of its node sorted by $h$. $\square$

Since the first goal is often found early and $U$ only decreases,
DFBnB is an "anytime" search, which can be stopped with the best path
found so far; on a graph with cycles, however, the tree of its paths is
infinite, and a depth bound (or an initial $U$) is needed for it to end.
On a graph, moreover, DFBnB expands a state once per path that reaches it
within the bound, the defect that the next section treats.

## 4.4 Iterative deepening {#sec-4-4}

Depth-first iterative deepening (DFID, Korf
[[7]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-7)) runs a depth-first search bounded by the
depth $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ until a goal is found; with unit costs the first
goal found is at the least depth, as with breadth-first search, while the
memory is that of the current path. IDA$^*$, also of Korf
[[7]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-7), replaces the depth by $f = g + h$ and
raises the bound, at each iteration, to the least $f$ that exceeded the
previous one; it is DFID when $h = 0$ and the costs are 1
([Corollary 4.6](#cor-4-6)).

### The interface

`IDAStar` (`IDAStar.h`) searches a `Domain` with IDA$^*$; its
declarations, without the bodies, are

```cpp
template< Domain D >
class IDAStar {
 public:
 explicit IDAStar( const D & domain , const SearchOptions & options = {} );
 [[nodiscard]] Solution< state_t< D > , cost_t< D > > solve( void ) const;
 };

template< Domain D >
[[nodiscard]] Solution< state_t< D > , cost_t< D > >
idastar( const D & domain , const SearchOptions & options = {} );
```

and `idastar( domain , options )` is
`IDAStar< D >( domain , options ).solve()`. The `Solution` and the
`SearchOptions` are those of [§1.5](01-introduction.md#sec-1-5). Of the
options, `node_limit` bounds the expansions (summed over the iterations), and
the search stops with `Status::node_limit` and an empty path when it reaches
it; `expected_nodes` is not used, since there is no CLOSED. The status is
`solved` with the cost and the path of the goal, or `unsolvable` when an
iteration cuts no node. In the counters of the `Solution`, `expanded` counts
every expansion, those repeated in later iterations included, `generated`
every successor that the domain passes (those cut by the bound included, and,
in a domain that is not an `OperatorDomain`, the moves back to the parent too,
which an `OperatorDomain` does not pass at all), and `stored` the largest
number of states held on the path ([Proposition 4.8](#prop-4-8)). In
particular, one obtains DFID by giving `IDAStar` a `Domain` whose heuristic
returns 0.

### IDA$^*$

Each iteration of IDA$^*$ is a depth-first search from $s_0$ that cuts
all the nodes with $f > \beta$, the bound $\beta$ being $h(s_0)$ in the
first iteration and, in each of the others, the least $f$ that exceeded
the bound of the previous one; the search ends when an iteration enters
a goal, or with no goal reachable when no node was cut.
[Figure 4.2](#fig-4-2) shows the bounds as nested contours of $f$.

![Figure 4.2. The bounds of IDA$^*$ as nested contours of $f$ around
$s_0$: the first iteration searches the paths whose nodes have $f \le
\beta_1 = h(s_0)$, each later one the paths within the least $f$ that
the previous iteration cut, and the last one, with $\beta = C^*$,
reaches the goal $t$ along an optimal
path.](../figures/memory-bounded-contours.svg){#fig-4-2}

The implementation makes the test of the bound when a successor is
generated, before entering it, and the goal test when a node is entered;
we say that a node is *entered* when the depth-first search is called on
it (and *expanded* when its successors are generated, which is the case
of every node entered that is not a goal), and *cut* when it is
generated with $f > \beta$ and not entered. Also, the successors are
those of `for_each_child` ([§1.5](01-introduction.md#sec-1-5)), passed
one at a time, without the move back to the *parent on the path*: by its
operator in an `OperatorDomain`, whose successor function skips the
inverse of the operator that generated the state, and otherwise by
comparing the successor with the state two places back on the path. The
following pseudocode is the code of `IDAStar::solve()` (`IDAStar.h`),
without the node limit and the counters.

**Algorithm 4.3** (IDA$^*$).

```text
 1  procedure IDA*(s₀)
 2     β ← h(s₀);  path ← (s₀)
 3     loop
 4        β' ← ∞
 5        if DFS(s₀, 0, β) then return the path, of cost the g of its end
 6        if β' = ∞ then return unsolvable
 7        β ← β'

 1  function DFS(u, g, β)                     ▷ u entered, g + h(u) ≤ β
 2     if u is a goal then return true
 3     for each successor v of u but its parent on the path do
 4        if g + c(u, v) + h(v) > β then         ▷ v cut, not entered
 5           β' ← min(β', g + c(u, v) + h(v))
 6        else
 7           append v to the path
 8           if DFS(v, g + c(u, v), β) then return true
 9           remove v from the path
10     return false
```

When a goal is entered, line 8 returns at once, the remaining
successors of each node on the path being skipped, and the path is left
from $s_0$ to the goal. The node limit is tested when a node that is not
a goal is entered, before its expansion; a goal is thus found even when
the limit has just been reached. [Figure 4.3](#fig-4-3) follows two
iterations on a small tree. We now prove the properties of the
algorithm (note that the proofs do not need a consistent heuristic),
assuming an admissible heuristic, a finite branching factor and costs
bounded below by some $\varepsilon > 0$.

![Figure 4.3. Two iterations of IDA$^*$. With $\beta = 4$ (left) the
search enters $s_0$ and $a$ and cuts the four nodes in the rust tint,
whose $f$ is written by them; the next bound is the least of them, 5.
With $\beta = 5$ (right) the search enters $e$ and reaches the goal $t$
with $g = 5 = \beta$; the nodes $b$ and $c$ are not generated
again, since the iteration stops at the
goal.](../figures/memory-bounded-ida-iter.svg){#fig-4-3}

<a id="lem-4-4"></a>**Lemma 4.4** (The bounds of IDA$^*$). If a goal is
reachable, the bounds of the iterations increase strictly and none of
them exceeds $C^*$; furthermore, an iteration with $\beta < C^*$ cuts at
least one node with $f \le C^*$.

*Proof.* The first bound is $h(s_0) \le C^*$, by admissibility. Consider
an iteration with bound $\beta < C^*$, and an optimal path $P = (s_0 =
u_0, \ldots, u_k)$. The depth-first search enters $s_0$ with $g = 0 =
g^*(s_0)$. Suppose that it enters some $u_j$, $j < k$, with $g =
g^*(u_j)$, along some path of that cost. Then it generates $u_{j+1}$,
with $g = g^*(u_{j+1})$, unless $u_{j+1}$ is the parent $w$ of $u_j$ on
the path held. This is not the case: $w$ has $g^*(w) \le g^*(u_j) - c(w, u_j) <
g^*(u_j)$, whereas $g^*(u_{j+1}) = g^*(u_j) + c(u_j, u_{j+1}) >
g^*(u_j)$, and hence $u_{j+1} \ne w$ (with an `OperatorDomain` the
state skipped is $w$ too, since the inverse of an operator leads back to
the state it came from, cf. [§1.5](01-introduction.md#sec-1-5)). Thus
$u_{j+1}$ is either cut or entered with $g = g^*(u_{j+1})$. The search
cannot enter $u_k$, since $f(u_k) = C^* > \beta$, and an iteration that
enters some other goal first ends the search (which is then not of the
form considered here); also, an iteration that enters no goal visits
the whole tree of its paths within the bound, so that the generation
above does take place. Hence some $u_{j+1}$ is cut, with $f(u_{j+1}) =
g^*(u_{j+1}) + h(u_{j+1}) \le C^*$ by admissibility. The next bound is
the least $f$ cut, which exceeds $\beta$ (only nodes with $f > \beta$
are cut) and does not exceed $f(u_{j+1}) \le C^*$. $\square$

<a id="thm-4-5"></a>**Theorem 4.5** (Optimality of IDA$^*$). If $h$ is
admissible and a goal is reachable, Algorithm 4.3 returns a path of cost
$C^*$.

*Proof.* A goal $t$ is entered only within the bound, i.e., with $g(t) +
h(t) \le \beta$; by admissibility $h(t) = 0$, and by
[Lemma 4.4](#lem-4-4) $\beta \le C^*$, so that $g(t) \le C^*$, and hence
$g(t) = C^*$, since $g(t)$ is the cost of a path. It remains to see that
some iteration does enter a goal. By [Lemma 4.4](#lem-4-4) the bounds
increase strictly within the values of $f$ not larger than $C^*$ taken
by the paths from $s_0$, of which there are finitely many (a path of
cost at most $C^*$ has at most $C^* / \varepsilon$ edges, and the
branching is finite), and an iteration with $\beta = C^*$ cannot cut a
node of an optimal path, by the argument of the lemma, and therefore it
enters the goal of one, if no goal was entered before. $\square$

<a id="cor-4-6"></a>**Corollary 4.6** (DFID). With $h = 0$ and unit
costs, the bounds of Algorithm 4.3 are $0, 1, 2, \ldots$, the iteration
of bound $\beta$ is the depth-first search to the depth $\beta$, and the
goal returned is at the least depth.

*Proof.* With $h = 0$ and unit costs, $f$ is the depth; a node cut by
the iteration of bound $\beta$ is at depth $\beta + 1$, and hence each
bound exceeds the previous one by 1 (an iteration that cuts no node
ends the search). The least depth of a goal is $C^*$, and the claim
follows from [Theorem 4.5](#thm-4-5). $\square$

<a id="prop-4-7"></a>**Proposition 4.7** (Termination and number of
iterations). (i) Each iteration ends, since it visits only paths of cost
at most $\beta$, which have at most $\beta / \varepsilon$ edges. (ii)
With integer costs and a reachable goal, IDA$^*$ makes at most $C^* -
h(s_0) + 1$ iterations; on the sliding-tile puzzles with the Manhattan
distance, at most $(C^* - h(s_0)) / 2 + 1$. (iii) If no goal is
reachable, IDA$^*$ reports it on a finite acyclic graph; on a graph with
a reachable cycle that the parent pruning does not remove (e.g., a cycle
of at least three states) it does not terminate, unless the node limit
stops it.

*Proof.* (i) is the bound on the length of a path, together with the
finite branching. (ii) By [Lemma 4.4](#lem-4-4) the bounds are integers
that increase strictly from $h(s_0)$ to at most $C^*$. On the puzzles a
move changes $g$ by 1 and the Manhattan distance by $\pm 1$; hence, $f$
changes by 0 or 2, and all the values of $f$ have the parity of
$h(s_0)$, so that the bound increases by at least 2. (iii) On a finite
acyclic graph the paths from $s_0$ are finitely many; once the bound is
at least the largest $f$ on them, no node is cut, $\beta' = \infty$, and
line 6 reports the instance unsolvable. On a graph with a reachable
cycle that the parent pruning does not remove, the paths that follow the
cycle again and again are not pruned and have unbounded cost; for any
$\beta$, the first node with $f > \beta$ on such a path is generated
from an entered node and cut, and hence $\beta' < \infty$ at every
iteration. (A cycle $u, v, u$ of two states is removed by the parent
pruning, so that, e.g., on a finite undirected tree without a goal the
search does terminate.) $\square$

<a id="prop-4-8"></a>**Proposition 4.8** (The memory of IDA$^*$). At
any time, Algorithm 4.3 holds the path, of at most $\lfloor \beta /
\varepsilon \rfloor + 1$ states, and one frame of the recursion per
state of the path; the successors are not stored, since they are passed
one at a time by the successor function of the domain (which may keep,
in each frame, the state of its own enumeration). If a goal is reachable,
the path never holds more than $\lfloor C^* / \varepsilon \rfloor + 1$
states; with unit costs and $s_0$ not a goal, the counter `stored` of a
solved search is $C^* + 1$.

*Proof.* Every state on the path is entered, with $g \le \beta$, and the
path has at most $\beta / \varepsilon$ edges; by [Lemma 4.4](#lem-4-4)
$\beta \le C^*$. With unit costs a path of an iteration has at most $C^*$
edges, and the path returned has exactly $C^*$ ([Theorem
4.5](#thm-4-5)); `stored` is the largest length of the path, measured
after each state appended at line 7 of DFS, and hence it is $C^* + 1$
when at least one state is appended, i.e., when $s_0$ is not a goal.
$\square$

The test `idastar` checks `stored` $= C^* + 1$ on three instances of the
15-puzzle of Korf, the costs against the exact distances of 100 states
of the 8-puzzle and against A$^*$ on small grids and on the map of
Romania, the report of an acyclic graph as unsolvable, and the stop at
1000 expansions. In fact, the cost of the repetitions is usually rather
small, as the next proposition shows, and the bound in (ii) of
[Proposition 4.7](#prop-4-7) is the one of Exercise 4.5.

<a id="prop-4-9"></a>**Proposition 4.9** (The overhead of the
iterations). Let $N_k$ be the expansions of the iteration $k$, and let
$N_{k+1} \ge b' N_k$ for some $b' > 1$ and all $k$. Then the expansions
of the $K$ iterations sum to at most $N_K \, b' / (b' - 1)$.

*Proof.* $N_k \le N_K / b'^{K - k}$, and therefore $\sum_{k=1}^{K} N_k \le
N_K \sum_{j \ge 0} b'^{-j} = N_K \, b' / (b' - 1)$. $\square$

On a tree of uniform branching $b$ and unit costs, DFID has $b' = b$,
and the overhead of the iterations is a factor of about $b / (b - 1)$
over the last one (Korf [[7]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-7)); IDA$^*$ has a
similar behavior when the number of nodes within the bound grows
geometrically with it, as it is usually observed on the sliding-tile
puzzles (an empirical fact, which the hypothesis of the proposition
only models). In the worst case, however, each iteration adds a single
node, the $K$-th iteration expands $K$ nodes, and the total is $K (K +
1) / 2$, i.e., quadratic in the expansions of A$^*$ (e.g., with real
costs that are all different).

### Duplicate paths and parent pruning

A depth-first search detects no duplicates: a state reached by several
paths within the bound is expanded once per path in each iteration.
On the sliding-tile puzzles the short cycles are rare, and IDA$^*$ with a
pattern database is the method of Korf and Felner
[[22]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-22) for the 24-puzzle (cf.
[Chapter 5](05-heuristics.md#ch-5)); on the grids, on the contrary, a
cell is reached by very many paths ([Figure 4.4](#fig-4-4)). Korf and
Schultze give an order of magnitude for the 15-puzzle: a depth-first
search to the average depth of an optimal solution, 53 moves, with the
branching factor 2.13, would generate about $2.5 \cdot 10^{17}$ nodes,
against about $10^{13}$ states [[156]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-156).

![Figure 4.4. The number of shortest paths from the corner $s_0$ to each
cell of a $5 \times 5$ grid with 4 moves, i.e., $\binom{i+j}{i}$ for the
cell $(i, j)$: a depth-first search without duplicate detection enters
the far corner 70 times along the shortest paths only, and the counts
grow exponentially with the side of the
grid.](../figures/memory-bounded-paths.svg){#fig-4-4}

The library prunes only the move back to the parent on the path, as
described above. The pruning removes no optimal path, since an optimal
path with positive costs has no cycle (cf.
[Proposition 2.16](02-best-first.md#prop-2-16)), and the proof of
[Lemma 4.4](#lem-4-4) shows that it never skips the next state of an
optimal path.

### Transposition tables

> **Status — planned.** `IDAStar` has no transposition table.

A transposition table ([§3.10](03-data-structures.md#sec-3-10)) keeps,
in a memory of fixed size, some of the states visited in the current
iteration with the least $g$ at which each was entered; a state reached
again with a $g$ not smaller than the one in the table is not entered,
since everything below it was searched with a larger remaining budget
(Reinefeld and Marsland [[161]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-161) describe this
and further uses of the table across the iterations). The pruning
changes the least $f$ that is cut, which may skip some values of the
bound; the next proposition shows that the optimality survives.

<a id="prop-4-10"></a>**Proposition 4.10** (Transposition tables keep
IDA$^*$ optimal). Let IDA$^*$ skip, within an iteration, any state $v$
reached with $g \ge g_T(v)$, where $g_T(v)$ is the least $g$ with which
$v$ was entered earlier in the same iteration (and is kept in the table).
Then, with $h$ admissible, the bounds remain at most $C^*$, and the path
returned is optimal.

*Proof.* Let $P = (s_0 = u_0, \ldots, u_k)$ be an optimal path and
consider an iteration that enters no goal; let $j$ be the largest index
such that $u_j$ is entered, at some time of the iteration, with $g =
g^*(u_j)$ ($j \ge 0$, since $s_0$ is entered with $g = 0$), and $j < k$.
At that entry $u_{j+1}$ is generated with $g^*(u_{j+1})$ (it is not the
parent, as in [Lemma 4.4](#lem-4-4)). It is not entered with
$g^*(u_{j+1})$, by the choice of $j$; nor is it skipped, since a skip
needs an earlier entry with $g_T(u_{j+1}) \le g^*(u_{j+1})$, i.e., with
$g^*(u_{j+1})$, again excluded by the choice of $j$. Hence $u_{j+1}$ is
cut, with $\beta < f(u_{j+1}) \le C^*$. Thus each iteration that ends
without a goal has $\beta < C^*$ and gives a next bound in $(\beta,
C^*]$, which shows that the bounds increase strictly and stay at most
$C^*$; and an iteration with $\beta = C^*$ cannot cut $u_{j+1}$, so that
it enters a goal. The rest of the proof of [Theorem 4.5](#thm-4-5)
applies unchanged. $\square$

We remark that the pruning may make IDA$^*$ skip some intermediate
values of the bound, never a value above $C^*$; with a table of bounded
size, entries are replaced, which only reduces the pruning.

### Stochastic node caching

> **Status — planned.**

Miura and Ishida [[162]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-162) keep in a cache of
bounded size a random subset of the states, each generated state being
stored with a probability $p$ if it is not cached yet; the cache is then
filled mostly by the states that are generated often, i.e., by those
reached along many paths ([§3.10](03-data-structures.md#sec-3-10)). The
cached states are then used for duplicate detection, as in a
transposition table, and the proof of [Proposition 4.10](#prop-4-10)
applies unchanged, since it holds for any content of the table.

## 4.5 Recursive best-first search {#sec-4-5}

> **Status — planned.** The library has no RBFS.

Recursive best-first search (RBFS, Korf [[116]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-116))
keeps, like IDA$^*$, a single path, but with the siblings of its nodes,
and it expands the nodes in best-first order. Each node $n$ carries a
stored value $F(n)$, initially $f(n)$; the search below a node is
bounded by the least stored value of the alternatives found higher up,
and when the subtree of a node exceeds its bound, the subtree is dropped
and its least $f$ is backed up into $F(n)$, and the search can return to
it later without starting from scratch ([Figure 4.5](#fig-4-5)).

**Algorithm 4.4** (RBFS, as it is usually given after
[[116]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-116); $F(c_2) = \infty$ when $n$ has one
successor).

```text
 1  function RBFS(n, F(n), B)
 2     if f(n) > B then return f(n)
 3     if n is a goal then exit with the path
 4     if n has no successor then return ∞
 5     for each successor c of n do
 6        if f(n) < F(n) then F(c) ← max(F(n), f(c)) else F(c) ← f(c)
 7     sort the successors by F
 8     while F(c₁) ≤ B and F(c₁) < ∞ do
 9        F(c₁) ← RBFS(c₁, F(c₁), min(B, F(c₂)))
10        reinsert c₁ in the order by F
11     return F(c₁)

    RBFS(s₀, f(s₀), ∞)
```

Line 6 inherits the backed-up value of the parent: if $F(n) > f(n)$, the
subtree of $n$ was already explored up to $F(n)$, and its successors are
given at least that value.

<a id="prop-4-11"></a>**Proposition 4.11** (The memory of RBFS). At any
time, Algorithm 4.4 holds the nodes of the recursion and their
successors, i.e., at most $b$ nodes per level of the recursion, which is
$O(b D)$ for a recursion of depth $D$.

*Proof.* A call holds its node and the list of its successors with their
stored values (lines 5 to 10), and nothing else survives a return. $\square$

Korf [[116]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-116) also states that RBFS with an
admissible heuristic returns an optimal path and expands the new nodes
in best-first order; we report these results without proof, having read
only the abstract of the paper. Furthermore, its weakness is the same as
that of IDA$^*$, i.e., the regeneration of the dropped subtrees, which
may be frequent when the stored values of two siblings alternate as the
least.

![Figure 4.5. Two steps of RBFS. (a) The best child $a$ (with $f = 5$) is
explored under the bound 6, the value of its best sibling $c$; both
successors of $a$ exceed it, and the least, 8, becomes the stored value
$F(a)$. (b) The subtree of $a$ is dropped, and the search turns to $c$,
under the bound 7 of the next best sibling

## 4.6 External search with delayed duplicate detection {#sec-4-6}

IDA$^*$ saves the memory of CLOSED by giving up the detection of the
duplicates; an external search keeps it, but on the disk, where the time
of an access is many orders of magnitude larger than in memory when it is
random, and only somewhat larger when it is sequential. Hence the
duplicates are not looked up when a node is generated, but detected later
and in batches, by scanning files: this is the *delayed duplicate
detection* (DDD) of [§3.11](03-data-structures.md#sec-3-11), whose
hash-based form is due to Korf [[40]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-40).

### Breadth-first search on the disk

The simplest case is the breadth-first search with unit costs. On an
undirected graph the duplicates of a new layer are found in very few
layers, as we now show; on a directed graph they may be in any earlier
layer.

<a id="prop-4-12"></a>**Proposition 4.12** (Duplicates of a
breadth-first layer). On an undirected graph with unit costs, let $L_i$
be the states at distance $i$ from $s_0$. A successor of a state of
$L_i$ is in $L_{i-1}$, $L_i$ or $L_{i+1}$.

*Proof.* If $v$ is adjacent to $u \in L_i$, then $\delta(v) \le
\delta(u) + 1 = i + 1$ and, the graph being undirected, $\delta(u) \le
\delta(v) + 1$, i.e., $\delta(v) \ge i - 1$, $\delta$ denoting the
distance from $s_0$. $\square$

Hence, when the layer $L_{i+1}$ is generated from $L_i$, its duplicates
are removed by comparing it with $L_i$ and $L_{i-1}$ only, and the older
layers can be dropped (the frontier search of Korf et al., cf.
[[156]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-156)). The detection was first made by
sorting the file of the new layer and merging it with the previous ones
(sorting-based DDD); the hash-based DDD of Korf
[[40]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-40) avoids the sorting by two independent
hash functions, a first one that splits the nodes into files (all the
copies of a state are then in the same file), and a second one by which
each file, loaded in a table in memory, drops its copies
[[156]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-156). When the states have a perfect hash
into a range that fits in memory at 2 bits per state, the two-bit
breadth-first search of Korf [[26]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-26) keeps
instead, for each state, one of four values (not reached, in the current
layer, in the next one, older) in an array of 2-bit entries; the library
uses it to build the pattern databases
([§5.5](05-heuristics.md#sec-5-5)).

### A$^*$ with delayed duplicate detection: the interface

`DDDAStar` is the External A$^*$ of Edelkamp, Jabbar and Schrödl
[[39]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-39), with the hash-based DDD of Korf
[[40]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-40) in place of the external sorts. It
requires a `DiskDomain` (`DDDAStar.h:135`), i.e., a `Domain` with integer
costs, since a bucket is a pair of values, and trivially copyable
states, since they are written to the files as their bytes. Its
declarations, without the bodies, are

```cpp

class DDDAStar {

                    DDDOptions ddd = {} );
 [[nodiscard]] Solution< state_t< D > , cost_t< D > > solve( void ) const;
 };

template< DiskDomain D >
[[nodiscard]] Solution< state_t< D > , cost_t< D > >
ddd_astar( const D & domain , const SearchOptions & options = {} ,
           const DDDOptions & ddd = {} );
```

where `ddd_astar` is `DDDAStar< D >( domain , options , ddd ).solve()`, and
the options of the disk are

```cpp

```

i.e., the number $P$ of files per bucket (`partitions`, 64 by default,
and 0 is refused with `std::invalid_argument`), the directory of the
files (a directory of its own is made in it, or in the temporary
directory of the system when it is empty, and removed with its files at
the end, also when the search stops at the limit or throws), and the
bytes of the buffer of each file being written (`buffer_bytes`,
$2^{16}$). Of the `SearchOptions`, `node_limit` bounds the states
written to the closed files (the counter `stored`), checked after each
file, and `expected_nodes` is not used. In the counters, `expanded`
counts the states expanded, each once, `generated` the successors,
`duplicates` the copies dropped, and `stored` the states written to the
closed files, i.e., the distinct states closed.

### The buckets and the files

The nodes are kept in *buckets* $B(g, h)$, one per pair of values, and
each bucket is split into $P$ *files* by the hash of the state, the file
$p$ holding the states $v$ with $\mathrm{hash}(v) \bmod P = p$; all the
copies of a state with the same $g$ are thus in the same file, and all
those of the same state in the files of the same index $p$, since $h$ is
a function of the state. A record of a file is the state, the hash of
the state from which it was generated (its *parent*) and, in an
`OperatorDomain`, the operator that generated it. Each bucket has an
*open* part, the records appended to it and not yet examined, and a
*closed* part, the states of it that have been expanded. The buckets are
expanded by increasing $f = g + h$ and, for equal $f$, by increasing $g$
(i.e., the key of a bucket is the pair $(g + h, g)$, compared
lexicographically, cf. [Figure 4.6](#fig-4-6)).

![Figure 4.6. The buckets $(g, h)$ of DDD-A$^*$ on a domain with unit
costs in which $h$ changes by 1 at each move, with the lines of equal
$f$. The closed buckets carry their order of expansion (all those of
$f = 4$ by increasing $g$, then $(1, 5)$); the current bucket, 6, sends
its successors to $(3, 3)$, of the same $f$ and a larger $g$, and to $(3,
5)$, of $f = 8$; the goal is in the bucket
$(6, 0)$.](../figures/memory-bounded-buckets.svg){#fig-4-6}

**Algorithm 4.5** (DDD-A$^*$, the pseudocode of the header
`DDDAStar.h:23-36`).

```text
 1  procedure DDD-A*(s₀)
 2     OPEN(0, h(s₀)) ← {s₀}
 3     while some OPEN(g, h) is not empty do
 4        (g, h) ← the bucket of least g + h, and then of least g
 5        for p ← 0 to P − 1 do
 6           T ← the states of the file p of OPEN(g, h), without copies
 7           for each closed bucket (g', h) with g' < g do
 8              T ← T ∖ the file p of CLOSED(g', h)
 9           the file p of CLOSED(g, h) ← T
10           if T has a goal then return the path to it, of cost g
11           for each u in T and each successor v of u do
12              append (v, hash(u)) to the file hash(v) mod P of
                 OPEN(g + c(u, v), h(v))
13     return unsolvable
```

Lines 6 to 12 are the processing of one file, which the code does as
follows: the open file is loaded in a hash table of the memory, which
keeps the first record of each state; the closed files of the same $h$
are scanned, and the states found in them are removed from the table;
what is left is written as the closed file and expanded, each successor
being appended through a buffer per file of the buckets it goes to
([Figure 4.7](#fig-4-7)). The expansion also checks the two hypotheses
on which the correctness rests, and throws `std::logic_error` on an edge
of a cost that is not positive or on a successor of a smaller $f$ than
its parent, i.e., on a heuristic that is not consistent.

**Algorithm 4.6** (The file $p$ of the bucket $(g, h)$).

```text
 1  procedure FILE(g, h, p)
 2     T ← an empty hash table
 3     for each record (v, x, o) of the file p of OPEN(g, h) do
 4        if v ∈ T then count a duplicate else T[v] ← (x, o)
 5     delete the file p of OPEN(g, h)
 6     for each closed bucket (g', h) do                 ▷ all have g' < g
 7        for each record (v, ·, ·) of the file p of CLOSED(g', h) do
 8           if v ∈ T then remove v from T;  count a duplicate
 9     if T = ∅ then return
10     write the records of T as the file p of CLOSED(g, h)
11     if some state of T is a goal then
12        return TRACE(its record, g), of cost g             ▷ Algorithm 4.7
13     for each record (u, x, o) of T do
14        for each successor v of u but the inverse of o do
15           if c(u, v) ≤ 0 or g + c(u, v) + h(v) < g + h then
16              throw logic_error
17           append (v, hash(u), the operator of v) to the buffer of
18              the file hash(v) mod P of OPEN(g + c(u, v), h(v))
```

The buffers of the files written by a bucket are flushed when all its
$P$ files have been processed, and the buckets that they fill are then
added to the open ones; the node limit is tested after line 18 of each
file.

![Figure 4.7. One file $p$ of the bucket $(g, h)$. (1) The open file,
which may hold copies; (2) loaded in a table, which drops the copy of

At the end the path is traced back from the goal through the hashes of
the parents.

**Algorithm 4.7** (The path to the goal of the record $x$, closed with
the cost $g$).

```text
1  function TRACE(x, g)
2     π ← (the state of x)
3     while g ≠ 0 or the state of x ≠ s₀ do
4        p ← (the parent hash of x) mod P
5        y ← a record of the file p of some CLOSED(g', h'), g' < g,
6           whose state u has hash(u) = the parent hash of x and the
7           state of x as a successor of cost g − g'   ▷ else throw
8        x ← y;  g ← g';  prepend u to π
9     return π
```

### Correctness

We now prove that the search is correct. Throughout, let $h$ be
consistent ([Definition 2.2](02-best-first.md#def-2-2)) and 0 on the
goals (hence admissible, cf. [§2.4](02-best-first.md#sec-2-4)), the
costs positive integers, and the inverse of the operator of a state, in
an `OperatorDomain`, lead back to the state that generated it.

<a id="lem-4-13"></a>**Lemma 4.13** (The order of the buckets). A
successor of a state of the bucket $(g, h)$ goes to a bucket of a larger
key; hence no bucket receives a record after it has been expanded, and
the buckets are expanded by increasing key.

*Proof.* A successor $v$ of $u$ has $g(v) = g + c(u, v) > g$ and, by
consistency, $f(v) = g + c(u, v) + h(v) \ge g + h(u) = f(u)$; hence its
key $(f(v), g(v))$ is larger than $(f(u), g)$. The search takes the open
bucket of least key (line 4), and each bucket filled later has a larger
key than the one being expanded. $\square$

<a id="lem-4-14"></a>**Lemma 4.14** (Each state is closed once, at its
optimal $g$). Every state $v$ reachable from $s_0$ whose bucket
$(g^*(v), h(v))$ is expanded before the search stops is written to a
closed file exactly once, in that bucket, and each other copy of it is
dropped at line 4 or at line 8 of Algorithm 4.6; no state is written to
two closed files.

*Proof.* Each copy of a state $v$ carries the cost of a path to $v$,
hence $g \ge g^*(v)$; all the copies of $v$ have $h(v)$ and lie in the
files of index $\mathrm{hash}(v) \bmod P$; and by Lemma 4.13 the buckets
are expanded by increasing key $(g + h, g)$, so that among the buckets of
$h(v)$ the one of $g^*(v)$ comes first. We prove by induction on the
order of the expansions that, when a bucket $(g, h)$ is expanded, (a)
every state $v$ with $g^*(v) = g$ and $h(v) = h$ has a copy in it, and
(b) a state of it is written to its closed file only if $g = g^*(v)$ and
it was not written before. For (a), $s_0$ is put in $(0, h(s_0))$ by line
2 of Algorithm 4.5. Let $v \ne s_0$, and $w$ its predecessor on an optimal
path, so that $g^*(w) = g - c(w, v) < g$; the bucket $(g^*(w), h(w))$ has
a smaller key (Lemma 4.13) and was expanded before, and by (a) and (b)
for it $w$ was written to its closed file there and expanded (the search
did not stop, since it reached $(g, h)$). The successor that line 14 of
Algorithm 4.6 skips is the state $u$ from which the record kept for $w$
was generated, closed with some $g_u$ such that $g_u + c(u, w) =
g^*(w)$; hence $g^*(u) \le g_u < g^*(w) < g^*(v)$, and $u \ne v$. Thus
the expansion of $w$ appended $v$ with $g^*(v)$ to $(g, h)$. For (b), a
copy of $v$ with $g > g^*(v)$ is in a bucket that follows $(g^*(v), h)$,
which by (a) held a copy of $v$ and by (b) wrote $v$ to its closed file
of index $\mathrm{hash}(v) \bmod P$ (no earlier closed file of $h$ could
hold $v$, since it would have a smaller $g$); line 8 scans that file and
removes $v$. A state with $g = g^*(v)$ is not in an earlier closed file,
for the same reason, and line 4 keeps one record per state of a file.
Hence each state whose bucket $(g^*(v), h(v))$ is expanded is written
exactly once, there, and each other copy of it is dropped at line 4 or
at line 8. $\square$

<a id="thm-4-15"></a>**Theorem 4.15** (Correctness of DDD-A$^*$). If a
goal is reachable, Algorithm 4.5 returns a path of cost $C^*$ (when the
node limit does not stop it first); otherwise, on a finite graph, it
reports the instance unsolvable. In both cases it terminates, and each
state is expanded at most once.

*Proof.* By [Lemma 4.14](#lem-4-14) each state is written to a closed
file at most once, and it is expanded only after that, i.e., at most
once. Let a goal be reachable, and let $t$ be an optimal one: $t$ is
closed in the bucket $(C^*, 0)$ if the search reaches it, and before it
the open buckets are not exhausted, since by the induction of [Lemma
4.14](#lem-4-14) the copies of the states of an optimal path reach
their buckets. A goal closed earlier, in a bucket $(g, 0)$, has $f = g$,
and by Lemma 4.13 its bucket precedes $(C^*, 0)$ only if $g \le C^*$;
since $g \ge C^*$ for a goal, the goal returned at line 10 has $g =
C^*$. By [Proposition 4.17](#prop-4-17) the path returned costs $g$.
For the termination, the buckets with $f \le C^*$ are finitely many
(with integer costs, $g \le C^*$ and $h \le C^*$ in them). Each of
them receives finitely many records: a record is a successor of a state
closed in a bucket of smaller key, i.e., with $g \le C^*$; these states
have $g^* \le C^*$, are finitely many (the branching being finite and the
costs at least 1), and each is expanded once, with at most $b$
successors.
When no goal is reachable on a finite graph, the states are finitely
many and each is expanded once, and hence the open buckets are
eventually exhausted, and line 13 reports the instance unsolvable.
$\square$

The counters follow from [Lemma 4.14](#lem-4-14): each record of an open
file that is examined is either written to a closed file or dropped, and
hence one has $\mathit{generated} + 1 = \mathit{stored} +
\mathit{duplicates}$ on a search that ends without a goal, the 1 being
the root, and $\mathit{expanded} = \mathit{stored}$ (in a search that
stops at a goal the last file is closed but not expanded, and
$\mathit{expanded} \le \mathit{stored}$). The test `ddd` checks the
identity on a grid of $50 \times 50$ cells split by a wall, where the
search closes the 1250 cells it can reach, the costs against the exact
distances of 50 states of the 8-puzzle with 1, 7 and 64 partitions,
against the optimal cost of an instance of the 15-puzzle of Korf, against
A$^*$ on small grids, and on the map of Romania, the stop at the node
limit, the refusal of an inconsistent
heuristic and of no partition, and that no file is left on the disk.

### Memory, files and the path

<a id="prop-4-16"></a>**Proposition 4.16** (Memory and transfers of
DDD-A$^*$). At any time, the memory of Algorithm 4.5 holds (i) the hash
table of one file, sized for its records, copies included, and the
states left in it; (ii) a buffer of `buffer_bytes` bytes for each file
written by the current bucket, i.e., at most $P$ for each distinct pair
$(g + c(u, v), h(v))$ of its successors; (iii) the buffer of the file
being read; and (iv) the sets of the open and of the closed buckets, one
entry per bucket. Each record of an open file is read once, and the
closed file $p$ of a bucket $(g', h)$ is read once for each file $p$ of a
later bucket of the same $h$ that is not empty.

*Proof.* The table of a file and the states written from it (lines 2 to
10 of Algorithm 4.6) are released when the next file is processed; the
writers of a bucket live until its $P$ files are processed; the scans
read through a buffer; and line 6 of Algorithm 4.6 runs over all the
closed buckets of the same $h$, all with a smaller $g$ by Lemma 4.13.
$\square$

Hence the memory is about that of the largest file, i.e., about $1 / P$
of the largest bucket when the hash spreads the states evenly (an
assumption on the hash of the domain), plus the buffers; with the
defaults, a bucket whose successors go to $k$ buckets holds $64 k$
buffers of 64 KB, i.e., $4 k$ MB. The files of all the closed buckets
are kept to the end, since a duplicate may be in any of those with the
same $h$, and hence a closed file is read once for each later bucket of
its $h$. Of course, on the undirected graphs with unit costs one could
drop the older layers, as in [Proposition 4.12](#prop-4-12), which the
library does not do, since it assumes no locality of the domain. Note
also that the order by increasing $g$ within a layer of $f$, which
[Lemma 4.13](#lem-4-13) requires, is the opposite of the tie-breaking of
A$^*$ ([§2.8](02-best-first.md#sec-2-8)): the search expands every
state of the last layer $f = C^*$ that it generates before the bucket of
the goal, and it may expand many more nodes than A$^*$ when that layer
is large.

<a id="prop-4-17"></a>**Proposition 4.17** (The path). Under the
hypotheses of [Lemma 4.14](#lem-4-14), Algorithm 4.7 never throws,
terminates, and returns a path from $s_0$ to the goal of cost $g$; each
step reads at most all the closed files of one partition.

*Proof.* The record $x$ kept for a state $v$ closed with $g$ was
generated from a state $w$ that was closed in a bucket $(g_w, h(w))$
with $g_w + c(w, v) = g$, and hence $g_w < g$, in the file
$\mathrm{hash}(w) \bmod P$, which is kept to the end; thus a record $y$
as at lines 5 to 7 exists (it may be another state with the same hash,
which is still a predecessor of $v$ at the cost $g - g'$). The state $u$
of $y$ was closed with $g'$, and the invariant of the loop is that
$\pi$ is a path from the state of $x$ to the goal of cost $g_0 - g$,
$g_0$ being the cost of the goal. Since $g$ decreases strictly and is a
nonnegative integer, the loop ends; it ends only with $g = 0$ and the
state $s_0$, and then $\pi$ has the cost $g_0$. The only state closed
with $g = 0$ is $s_0$, since the costs are positive; hence the loop
cannot reach $g = 0$ elsewhere. $\square$

## 4.7 Symbolic search {#sec-4-7}

> **Status — planned.** The library has no symbolic search.

A symbolic search represents whole sets of states by their characteristic
functions. With the states coded by $n$ Boolean
variables $x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$, a set $S$ is the function
$\chi_S(x)$, true exactly on the codes of the states of $S$, and the
moves are a transition relation $\mathit{Trans}(x, x')$, true when the
state $x'$ is a successor of $x$. The image of a set is
$$\mathit{Img}(S)(x') = \exists x \, \big( \chi_S(x) \wedge
\mathit{Trans}(x, x') \big),$$
renamed from $x'$ to $x$; it is the set of the successors of the states
of $S$, computed in one operation on the functions. The functions are
represented by reduced ordered binary decision diagrams (BDDs), i.e.,
directed acyclic graphs in which each node tests a variable, in a fixed
order, with two successors for its values, and the leaves are 0 and 1
([Figure 4.8](#fig-4-8)). For a fixed order of the variables the reduced
ordered BDD of a function is unique (Bryant
[[163]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-163)); hence, the equality of two sets is a
comparison of two pointers when the BDDs share their nodes, and the
operations $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\neg$ take a time polynomial in the sizes
of the operands [[163]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-163); the size of a BDD,
however, depends heavily on the order, and may be exponential in $n$.

![Figure 4.8. The reduced ordered BDD of $\chi(x) = (x_1 \wedge x_2)
\vee x_3$ with the order $x_1 < x_2 < x_3$. A solid arc follows the
value 1 of the variable of its node, a dashed one the value 0; the node
$x_3$ is shared by the two paths that reach
it.](../figures/memory-bounded-bdd.svg){#fig-4-8}

**Algorithm 4.8** (Symbolic breadth-first search).

```text
1  procedure SYMBOLIC-BFS(s₀)
2     Reach ← {s₀};  Frontier ← {s₀};  d ← 0         ▷ as BDDs
3     loop
4        if Frontier ∩ Goals ≠ ∅ then return d
5        Frontier ← Img(Frontier) ∖ Reach
6        if Frontier = ∅ then return unsolvable
7        Reach ← Reach ∪ Frontier;  d ← d + 1
```

<a id="prop-4-18"></a>**Proposition 4.18** (Symbolic breadth-first
search). At line 4 of the iteration $d$, Frontier is the set of the
states at distance $d$ from $s_0$ (with unit costs) and Reach the set of
those at distance at most $d$; hence Algorithm 4.8 returns the least
distance of a goal, and on a finite space it terminates.

*Proof.* By induction on $d$. For $d = 0$ both sets are $\{s_0\}$. If
the claim holds for $d$, the successors of the states at distance $d$
that are not at distance at most $d$ are those at distance $d + 1$, which
is line 5, and line 7 adds them to Reach. The first $d$ at which the
frontier meets the goals is the least distance of a goal; on a finite
space Reach grows strictly until the frontier is empty. $\square$

One can rebuild the path backwards, by intersecting the preimage of the
goal reached with the frontiers kept for the purpose. Symbolic A$^*$
keeps instead one BDD per bucket of $(g, h)$, or of $(f, h)$, and
expands the buckets in the order of A$^*$, as DDD-A$^*$ does with its
files (cf. Edelkamp and Schrödl [[10]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-10)).

## 4.8 Novelty and width-based search {#sec-4-8}

> **Status — planned.** The library has no width-based search.

Let a state be described by the values of $m$ variables, and call an
*atom* a pair (variable, value). The *novelty* of a newly generated state
$s$ is the least $i$ such that some set of $i$ atoms true in $s$ is true
in no state generated before $s$. The search IW$(i)$ of Lipovetzky and
Geffner [[104]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-104) is a breadth-first search that
prunes each generated state of novelty larger than $i$; its memory is
that of the sets of at most $i$ atoms seen so far, and the iterated width
runs IW$(1)$, IW$(2)$, ... until a goal is found.

**Algorithm 4.9** (IW$(i)$).

```text
1  procedure IW(s₀, i)
2     Seen ← the sets of at most i atoms true in s₀;  Q ← (s₀)
3     while Q is not empty do
4        u ← the first node of Q, removed
5        if u is a goal then return the path to u
6        for each successor v of u do
7           if some set of at most i atoms of v is not in Seen then
8              add those sets to Seen;  append v to Q
9     return failure
```

<a id="prop-4-19"></a>**Proposition 4.19** (The size of IW$(i)$). With
$m$ variables of at most $D$ values each, IW$(i)$ appends at most
$\sum_{j=1}^{i} \binom{m}{j} D^j = O\big((m D)^i\big)$ states to Q.
Furthermore, the iterated width finds a goal, if one is reachable, at
the latest with $i = m$.

*Proof.* A state appended at line 8 adds at least one set of at most $i$
atoms to Seen, which never loses one; the sets of $j$ atoms over distinct
variables are at most $\binom{m}{j} D^j$. With $i = m$, a state not
generated before has the set of all its $m$ atoms unseen, since the $m$
atoms determine the state, while a state generated before has all its
sets in Seen; hence IW$(m)$ is a breadth-first search with duplicate
detection, which is complete on a finite space. $\square$

We remark that IW$(i)$ for small $i$ is neither complete nor optimal;
its interest is that many classical planning problems with single atomic
goals have a small width, and IW$(1)$ or IW$(2)$ solves them in time
polynomial in $m$ [[104]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-104) (cf.
[Chapter 16](16-planning.md#ch-16)).

## 4.10 Notes and references {#sec-4-10}

DFID and IDA$^*$ are due to Korf [[7]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-7), who
proved that DFID is asymptotically optimal among the brute-force tree
searches in time and space, and IDA$^*$ with a disjoint pattern database
is the search of Korf and Felner [[22]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-22) on the
24-puzzle; RBFS is also due to Korf [[116]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-116),
the transposition tables in iterative deepening were studied by
Reinefeld and Marsland [[161]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-161), and the
stochastic node caching by Miura and Ishida
[[162]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-162). The External A$^*$ is that of
Edelkamp, Jabbar and Schrödl [[39]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-39), the
hash-based DDD that of Korf [[40]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-40), applied by
Korf and Schultze to the complete breadth-first search of the 15-puzzle
[[156]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-156), and the two-bit breadth-first search
that of Korf [[26]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-26). The BDDs are due to Bryant
[[163]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-163), and the symbolic search with them and
the beam search are surveyed by Edelkamp and Schrödl
[[10]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-10); the width-based search is due to
Lipovetzky and Geffner [[104]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-104).

As to where the library stands, the following facts are stated from the
code. `IDAStar` is the algorithm of [[7]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-7) with
the test of the bound at the generation and the parent pruning of
[§2.9](02-best-first.md#sec-2-9), and it runs with the pattern
databases of [Chapter 5](05-heuristics.md#ch-5) on the 24-puzzle (the
README reports, for 50 ends of random walks of 150 moves, 49 instances
solved within $10^{10}$ expansions each, a count that does not depend on
the machine). `DDDAStar` uses the hash-based detection rather than the
sorting of [[39]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-39), but it keeps all the closed
files and does not implement the frontier search, nor a parallel
expansion of the files as in [[156]](Z-bibliography.md#ref-156); its order
by increasing $g$ within a layer may cost many more expansions than
A$^*$ in the last layer. Beam search, the depth-first branch-and-bound of
a `Domain`, the transposition tables and the stochastic node caching of
IDA$^*$, RBFS, the symbolic search and the width-based search are not in
the library.
